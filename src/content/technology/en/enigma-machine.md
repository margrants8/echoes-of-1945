Enigma was an electro-mechanical cipher machine used by Nazi Germany to encrypt military and diplomatic communications. Employing a system of rotating wheels and a plugboard, it scrambled each letter into a different one, producing a cipher the Germans believed to be effectively unbreakable. Variants were used by the army, navy, and air force throughout the war.

Polish cryptographers of the Cipher Bureau, led by Marian Rejewski, achieved the first breaks into Enigma in the 1930s and shared their methods with Britain and France shortly before the war. This work was continued and greatly expanded at Bletchley Park in Britain, where Alan Turing and colleagues developed the electro-mechanical "bombe" machines to help recover daily settings.

The intelligence derived from broken Enigma traffic, code-named Ultra, gave the Allies crucial insight into German operations, notably in the Battle of the Atlantic. Historians generally agree that this codebreaking effort shortened the war, though its precise impact is a matter of scholarly discussion.

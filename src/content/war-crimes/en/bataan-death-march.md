The Bataan Death March was the forced transfer of Filipino and American prisoners of war by the Imperial Japanese Army following the surrender of Allied forces on the Bataan Peninsula in the Philippines on 9 April 1942. Tens of thousands of prisoners, weakened by months of fighting, disease, and short rations, were compelled to march roughly 100 kilometers northward toward railheads and prison camps.

During the march, prisoners endured extreme heat, denial of food and water, and physical abuse. Those who fell behind or were unable to continue were frequently beaten, bayoneted, or shot by guards. Thousands died along the route, and many more perished in the weeks that followed at Camp O'Donnell and other holding sites; exact death tolls are uncertain and vary between sources.

After the war, the treatment of the prisoners was among the matters examined in war crimes proceedings, and the Japanese commander held responsible for the Philippines campaign was tried and executed. The Bataan Death March remains a widely documented example of the mistreatment of prisoners of war during the Pacific War, preserved through survivor testimony, military records, and postwar investigations.

The International Military Tribunal for the Far East, commonly known as the Tokyo Trial, was convened by the Allied powers to prosecute senior leaders of the Empire of Japan for crimes committed before and during the Second World War. The tribunal's proceedings opened in Tokyo on 3 May 1946 and continued until November 1948, with judges drawn from eleven Allied nations and the Australian jurist Sir William Webb presiding.

Twenty-eight defendants were charged with crimes against peace, conventional war crimes, and crimes against humanity. The tribunal heard testimony and documentary evidence concerning aggression, the treatment of prisoners of war, and atrocities committed in occupied territories. In its judgment, the tribunal convicted the defendants who remained on trial; seven were sentenced to death, including former Prime Minister Hideki Tojo, and were executed in December 1948, while others received prison terms.

Modeled in part on the Nuremberg proceedings in Europe, the Tokyo Trial contributed to the development of international criminal law, though it has also been the subject of scholarly debate over its procedures, selection of defendants, and omissions. Its extensive records remain an important source for the study of the Pacific War and postwar justice.

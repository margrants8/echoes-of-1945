The Nanjing Massacre was a period of mass killing and widespread sexual violence committed by Imperial Japanese Army forces after they captured Nanjing, then the capital of the Republic of China, on 13 December 1937. Over roughly six weeks, Japanese troops killed large numbers of surrendered soldiers and unarmed civilians and carried out extensive looting, arson, and rape across the city.

Foreign residents who remained in Nanjing organized the Nanjing Safety Zone in an effort to shelter civilians, and their diaries, letters, and reports provide some of the contemporaneous documentation of events. After the war, the atrocities were examined by the International Military Tribunal for the Far East and the Nanjing War Crimes Tribunal, both of which prosecuted responsible commanders.

The scale of the killing remains a subject of scholarly and political dispute, with credible estimates commonly ranging from roughly 200,000 to more than 300,000 dead. The massacre continues to be studied through tribunal records, survivor testimony, and archival materials, and it remains a significant subject in the history and memory of the Second Sino-Japanese War.

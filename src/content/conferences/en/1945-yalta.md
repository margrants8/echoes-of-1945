The Yalta Conference, held February 4–11, 1945, brought the Big Three together in the Crimean resort as Nazi Germany neared collapse. With victory in Europe imminent, the discussions turned decisively to the shape of the post-war world.

The leaders agreed to divide Germany into occupation zones — American, British, and Soviet, with a French zone carved from the western Allies' shares — and to pursue denazification and demilitarization. Stalin secured territorial concessions in exchange for a pledge to enter the war against Japan within three months of Germany's surrender. The three powers also agreed to convene the United Nations founding conference in San Francisco in April 1945.

The Declaration on Liberated Europe promised free elections in liberated nations, but its vague terms and the Soviet military presence in Eastern Europe meant the pledge was largely unfulfilled. Yalta has since been debated as both a pragmatic wartime bargain and, by critics, as a concession that consigned Eastern Europe to Soviet domination.

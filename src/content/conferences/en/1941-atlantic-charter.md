The Atlantic Charter was a joint declaration issued on August 14, 1941, by President Franklin D. Roosevelt and Prime Minister Winston Churchill following a secret shipboard meeting in Placentia Bay off Newfoundland. The United States was not yet a belligerent, but the two leaders set out a shared vision of the aims of the war against Nazi Germany and of the peace they hoped would follow.

The charter enumerated a set of common principles: the two nations sought no territorial gains, opposed territorial changes made against the wishes of the peoples concerned, respected the right of all peoples to choose their own form of government, and favored freer trade, economic collaboration, freedom of the seas, and a lasting system of general security. It notably invoked the goal of a world free from want and fear.

Though it was a statement of principle rather than a binding treaty, the Atlantic Charter proved influential. Its language was incorporated into the Declaration by United Nations of January 1942 and helped lay the ideological groundwork for the postwar United Nations. It also raised expectations of self-determination that would resonate in later movements toward decolonization.

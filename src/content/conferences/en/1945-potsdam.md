The Potsdam Conference, held July 17 to August 2, 1945, was the last of the wartime Big Three summits. Germany had surrendered in May, and the cast had changed: Harry S. Truman had succeeded the late Roosevelt, and Clement Attlee replaced Churchill mid-conference after Labour won the British general election.

The powers set out the "Four Ds" for occupied Germany — demilitarization, denazification, democratization, and decartelization — and established a Council of Foreign Ministers to prepare peace treaties. They provisionally accepted the Oder–Neisse line as Poland's western frontier and agreed that each power would take reparations largely from its own occupation zone, a formula that foreshadowed the economic division of Germany.

From Potsdam, Truman, Churchill, and Chiang Kai-shek issued the Potsdam Declaration, demanding Japan's unconditional surrender under threat of "prompt and utter destruction." Japan's rejection preceded the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki. Tensions over Eastern Europe at Potsdam are often cited as an early marker of the emerging Cold War.

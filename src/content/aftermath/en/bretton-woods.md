The United Nations Monetary and Financial Conference, commonly known as the Bretton Woods Conference, convened delegates from 44 Allied nations at Bretton Woods, New Hampshire, from July 1 to July 22, 1944. Meeting while the war still raged, the delegates sought to design a stable international economic order that would avoid the competitive devaluations and trade barriers blamed for the economic disasters of the interwar years.

The conference produced agreements establishing two lasting institutions: the International Monetary Fund, charged with overseeing exchange-rate stability and providing short-term support to countries facing balance-of-payments difficulties, and the International Bank for Reconstruction and Development, later the core of the World Bank Group, tasked with financing reconstruction and development. The negotiations were shaped above all by the economist John Maynard Keynes of Britain and Harry Dexter White of the United States.

The resulting Bretton Woods system tied participating currencies to the U.S. dollar, which was in turn convertible to gold at a fixed rate. This framework underpinned the expansion of international trade and finance in the post-war decades until the United States ended dollar-gold convertibility in 1971. The IMF and the World Bank continue to operate as central institutions of the global economy.

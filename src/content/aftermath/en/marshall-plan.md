The Marshall Plan, formally the European Recovery Program, was the principal United States initiative to aid the rebuilding of Western European economies after the Second World War. It grew from a speech delivered by Secretary of State George C. Marshall at Harvard University on June 5, 1947, in which he offered American assistance to a Europe struggling with economic dislocation, food shortages, and political instability. President Harry S. Truman signed the enabling legislation, the Economic Cooperation Act, into law on April 3, 1948.

Over roughly four years the program channeled more than 13 billion dollars in economic and technical assistance to participating nations, which coordinated their requests through the Organisation for European Economic Co-operation. The aid was used to purchase goods, modernize industry, and restore trade. The Soviet Union and the states of Eastern Europe declined to participate under pressure from Moscow.

The plan is widely credited with accelerating Western Europe's recovery, restoring industrial and agricultural output, and fostering economic cooperation among former adversaries. It also deepened the division between the Western bloc and the Soviet sphere, becoming an early instrument of the emerging Cold War. Marshall was awarded the Nobel Peace Prize in 1953, in part for the program that bore his name.

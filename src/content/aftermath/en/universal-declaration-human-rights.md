The Universal Declaration of Human Rights was adopted by the United Nations General Assembly in Paris on December 10, 1948. The vote was 48 in favor, none against, with eight abstentions. The date is now marked each year as Human Rights Day. The document was drafted in the aftermath of the atrocities of the Second World War, as the international community sought to articulate a common standard of rights for all peoples.

The declaration was prepared by a drafting committee of the Commission on Human Rights, chaired by Eleanor Roosevelt of the United States, with important contributions from figures such as René Cassin of France, Charles Malik of Lebanon, and Peng-chun Chang of China. Its thirty articles set out a broad range of civil, political, economic, social, and cultural rights, beginning with the assertion that all human beings are born free and equal in dignity and rights.

Although the declaration is not itself a legally binding treaty, it has become one of the most influential documents of the modern era. Its principles have shaped national constitutions, informed subsequent binding covenants, and provided a foundation for the international human rights framework. It has been translated into hundreds of languages and remains a defining reference point for the post-war international order.

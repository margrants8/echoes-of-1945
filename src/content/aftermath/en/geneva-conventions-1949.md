The Geneva Conventions of 1949 are a set of four international treaties, adopted on August 12, 1949, that form the core of modern international humanitarian law. They were drafted at a diplomatic conference in Geneva convened in the aftermath of the Second World War, whose vast civilian suffering and mistreatment of prisoners had exposed the inadequacy of earlier agreements.

The four conventions address distinct categories of war victims: the first protects wounded and sick soldiers on land, the second protects wounded, sick, and shipwrecked members of armed forces at sea, the third governs the treatment of prisoners of war, and the fourth, a major innovation, protects civilians in time of war, including those in occupied territory. A common Article 3 extended basic humane protections to non-international armed conflicts.

The conventions have been ratified universally, making them among the most widely accepted treaties in the world. They were later supplemented by Additional Protocols adopted in 1977. Administered with the support of the International Committee of the Red Cross, this framework remains the foundational legal standard for the conduct of armed conflict.

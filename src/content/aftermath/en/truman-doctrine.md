The Truman Doctrine was the foreign-policy principle announced by President Harry S. Truman in an address to a joint session of the United States Congress on March 12, 1947. Prompted by Britain's declaration that it could no longer sustain military and economic support to Greece and Turkey, Truman asked Congress to appropriate 400 million dollars in aid to the two governments, which he portrayed as threatened by communist pressure.

In his speech Truman framed the request in broad terms, declaring that it must be the policy of the United States "to support free peoples who are resisting attempted subjugation by armed minorities or by outside pressures." This statement extended American commitments beyond a single crisis and signaled a willingness to intervene against the spread of Soviet influence worldwide.

The doctrine is widely regarded as a founding moment of the Cold War and of the American strategy of containment. It set a precedent for later initiatives such as the Marshall Plan and the formation of NATO, marking a decisive break from the interwar tradition of avoiding permanent overseas entanglements.

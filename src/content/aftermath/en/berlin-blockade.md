The Berlin Blockade was one of the first major crises of the Cold War. On June 24, 1948, the Soviet Union cut off road, rail, and canal access to the western sectors of Berlin, which lay deep within the Soviet occupation zone of Germany. The move came in response to the Western Allies' introduction of a new currency, the Deutsche Mark, and their moves toward creating a West German state, and it left more than two million people in the western sectors without a land supply route.

Rather than abandon the city or force the blockade by ground, the United States and the United Kingdom organized a massive airlift to supply West Berlin by air. Beginning in late June 1948, transport aircraft flew around the clock into the city's airfields, delivering food, coal, and other essentials. At its height the airlift landed thousands of tons of supplies each day.

The Soviet Union lifted the blockade on May 12, 1949, after the airlift demonstrated that the Western powers could sustain the city indefinitely. The confrontation hardened the division of Germany, contributed to the creation of the Federal Republic of Germany and the German Democratic Republic later that year, and reinforced the Western resolve that led to the formation of NATO.

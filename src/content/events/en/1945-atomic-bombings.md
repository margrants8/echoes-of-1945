On 6 August 1945, a United States B-29 bomber dropped an atomic bomb on the Japanese city of Hiroshima, the first use of a nuclear weapon in war. Three days later, on 9 August, a second bomb was dropped on Nagasaki. The two attacks came in the final weeks of the Pacific war, following the Allied demand for Japan's unconditional surrender.

Both cities were largely destroyed by the blast, heat, and resulting fires. Tens of thousands of people were killed instantly or within hours, and many more died over the following weeks and months from injuries and radiation-related illness. Casualty estimates vary considerably among sources: figures for deaths by the end of 1945 are commonly given in the range of roughly 90,000 to 146,000 for Hiroshima and about 39,000 to 80,000 for Nagasaki, with further deaths in later years. Precise totals remain uncertain and disputed.

Japan announced its surrender on 15 August 1945, with the formal signing on 2 September, ending the Second World War. The bombings, together with the Soviet declaration of war on Japan, are central to long-running historical debate over the causes of Japan's surrender and the use of nuclear weapons, and they inaugurated the nuclear age.

The Battle of Iwo Jima began on February 19, 1945, when U.S. Marines landed on the small volcanic island roughly midway between the Mariana Islands and the Japanese home islands. The island's airfields were valued as a base for fighter escorts and as an emergency landing site for bombers striking Japan, making its capture a priority for American planners.

The Japanese garrison, commanded by General Tadamichi Kuribayashi, had turned the island into a dense network of tunnels, bunkers, and fortified positions, and largely forsook the beaches to fight from concealed defenses inland. The result was a brutal, weeks-long struggle across volcanic terrain. The raising of the American flag atop Mount Suribachi on February 23 produced one of the war's most famous photographs, though heavy fighting continued for another month.

Nearly the entire Japanese garrison of some 21,000 men was killed, with only a few hundred taken prisoner. American forces suffered roughly 26,000 casualties, including about 6,800 dead — among the costliest battles in Marine Corps history. The island was declared secure on March 26, 1945.

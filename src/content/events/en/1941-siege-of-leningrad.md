The Siege of Leningrad began on 8 September 1941, when German forces, aided by Finnish troops to the north, completed the encirclement of the city during the opening months of the invasion of the Soviet Union. Rather than storm Leningrad directly, the Germans sought to strangle it through blockade and bombardment, cutting the city off from overland supply.

The first winter of 1941–42 was catastrophic. With food stocks exhausted and rations reduced to a few hundred grams of bread a day, hundreds of thousands of civilians died of starvation, cold, and disease. A tenuous lifeline was maintained across the ice of Lake Ladoga, the so-called Road of Life, over which supplies were brought in and civilians evacuated.

Soviet forces partially broke the blockade in January 1943 and fully lifted it on 27 January 1944, after roughly 872 days. The siege is remembered as one of the longest and most destructive in modern history. Civilian deaths are usually estimated at around a million, and by some accounts considerably more, in addition to heavy military losses on both sides.
